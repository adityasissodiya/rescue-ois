\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Single Writer Instead of CRDTs}

Local-first research makes a strong case for replica autonomy and conflict-free merging in collaborative systems~\cite{kleppmann2019localfirst,haas2023lore,kohler2025consistent,jannes2021owebsync}. That logic does not by itself satisfy R3. Incident state contains command decisions, ordered status transitions, and resource assignments for which CRDT-based merge has no defensible application semantics: there is no commutative merge function for a status transition that two responders attempt to drive in different directions, and any resolution rule that picks one of them implicitly invents the authority that R3 requires be explicit. The single-writer design adopted here makes that authority operational rather than emergent, at the cost of write availability at responder nodes during partition.

\subsection{Threat Model and Security Scope}

The target deployment assumes an organizational CA or identity provider, enrolled devices and users, and backend trust in certificate-derived identity only when the reverse proxy and service boundary are correctly configured. It handles replayed field submissions through \texttt{client\_event\_id} idempotency and limits tablet reachability through local edge APIs and network segmentation.

Several threats remain outside the prototype evaluation. A stolen or compromised tablet can be revoked only when nodes regain contact with the authority infrastructure or receive an updated trust bundle; instantaneous revocation during total disconnection is not provided. A stolen vehicle node is more serious because it may hold cached data and, if it is the command node, operational authority; the architecture relies on device encryption, operational fencing, and audit rather than Byzantine tolerance. The design also does not address a compromised organizational CA, Byzantine command behavior, physical tamper resistance beyond deployment controls, or audit-log integrity against a fully compromised node. These are deployment and hardening questions, not properties established by the emulation.

\subsection{What This Paper Does Not Claim}
\label{sec:non-claims}

To keep the evaluation scope unambiguous, we state the claims this paper
does \emph{not} make. We do not claim production readiness or physical
field deployment, and in particular we do not claim physical Rajant mesh
behavior, measured WireGuard or mTLS overhead, or end-to-end validation
of the Android client. We do not claim full crash-safety at the service
level or a fully verified implementation: only the TLA+ specification
and the Hypothesis model are checked, and the Python service code is
not extracted from the model. We do not claim automatic failover --
promotion is operator-initiated -- and we do not propose Rescue OIS as
a replacement for Raft or Paxos, nor as generic offline-first database
or mesh-platform behavior. Byzantine fault tolerance is also outside
the threat model. The next planned experiment is a multi-host
\texttt{tc-netem} evaluation across two link-condition profiles ---
moderately stressed mesh (80\,ms~$\pm$~30\,ms delay, 2\,\% loss) and
degraded mesh (200\,ms~$\pm$~80\,ms delay, 8\,\%
loss)~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh,kumbhar2015legacy} --- informed by
mesh-network measurements in the cited references. Physical Rajant
mesh deployment with vehicle-mounted radios remains the longer-term
step.
