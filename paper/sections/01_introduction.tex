\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public-safety information systems are an instance of essential public services that must remain operational when commercial connectivity fails~\cite{wisniewski2023continuity, oecd2019resilience, karaman2024resilient}. Disasters routinely degrade or destroy commercial telecom infrastructure precisely when coordination demand is highest~\cite{marshall2023telecommunications}, and even routine rescue operations regularly span terrain where commercial coverage is intermittent or absent~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh, andreassen2020information}. The 2018 Swedish forest-fire crisis is a recent concrete example: large-scale rescue operations exposed substantial gaps in the digital-information support available to responders, gaps that the existing information-systems literature has documented in detail~\cite{murphy2021digital}.

In this operational class, rescue-service vehicles operate up to several hours away from regional dispatch centres, in terrain where wireless connectivity alternates between full wide-area connectivity, mesh-only operation, and total isolation~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh, wang2023overview}. Vehicles must continue acting on stale or partial information without external coordination, and field observations entered on tablets must never be lost when connectivity is unavailable for hours. Information loss or stale views in this setting has consequences in command-hierarchical environments: wrong assignments, missed hazards, and conflicting orders~\cite{allen2014information, andreassen2020information}.

A Swedish municipal rescue service, R\"addningstj\"ansten Storg\"oteborg (RSG), publishes operational guidance for incident planning and the structure of municipal rescue plans~\cite{rsg-rad201, rsg-kommunalplan}. That guidance distinguishes a generally available part of the rescue plan from a facility-specific part subject to secrecy under the Swedish Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act, and is explicit that incident-planning information is an ongoing process rather than a static paper artifact, that some plans must be handled in operational mobile IT support while others remain outside that support, and that plans contain layered information about buildings, risks, technical protection systems, and adjacent risks. Independent academic work on Swedish rescue-service IS practice has documented compatible end-user requirements and information-flow shortcomings~\cite{pilemalm2014enabling, westman2021mobilisation}. From these public sources we derive five operational requirements that drive the system in this paper: continued operation under disconnection from any other tier; durable forward-only submission of field observations; clear authority for incident-state decisions; predictable network behaviour without cross-vehicle broadcast hazards; and reuse of the rescue-service organisation's existing certificate and identity infrastructure rather than a parallel identity stack. We treat these as load-bearing requirements (Section~\ref{sec:requirements}) and derive every architectural choice in this paper from them.

No existing replication pattern satisfies this combination of requirements. Consensus-based state-machine replication~\cite{lamport1998parttime, ongaro2014raft, katsarakis2020hermes} is unavailable during the partitions the operational profile makes routine, because progress requires a quorum that cannot be assembled. Multi-primary replication for disconnected operation~\cite{mucha2023database} preserves local availability but resolves conflicts after partitions heal, which is incompatible with the requirement that incident-state decisions have a single, attributable authority at every instant. CRDT-based replication~\cite{kleppmann2019localfirst, almeida2014delta, kohler2025consistent, haas2023lore} is excellent for naturally mergeable state, but incident decisions are ordered authority claims for which commutative merge has no defensible application semantics. Each existing pattern fails on a different requirement; the gap is not a missing protocol but a missing combination.

This paper asks: \emph{how should authority-bearing data be synchronized across intermittently connected edge tiers, when the operational model already designates a single principal as authoritative?} It answers with a tiered synchronisation architecture combining a single-writer incident journal at the command vehicle, durable forward-only outboxes at responder vehicles and tablets, and explicit manual promotion. The contributions are (i) an operational-requirements derivation from publicly available Swedish rescue-service guidance; (ii) an architectural design in which every choice maps to one or more stated requirements; (iii) a synchronisation protocol with stated consistency guarantees; (iv) a prototype implementation; and (v) an emulation evaluation including safety verification and a baseline comparison.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} positions the design against state-machine replication, local-first systems, and edge-connectivity work. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} presents the operational requirements and the three-tier architecture that derives from them. Section~\ref{sec:sync} specifies the synchronisation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} summarises the prototype scaffold. Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports the evaluation, the formal safety verification, and the Raft baseline comparison.
