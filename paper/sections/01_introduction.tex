\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public-safety information systems must remain operational when commercial connectivity fails~\cite{wisniewski2023continuity, oecd2019resilience, karaman2024resilient}.
Rescue-service vehicles operate up to several hours from regional dispatch centres in terrain where wireless connectivity alternates between full wide-area coverage, mesh-only operation, and total isolation~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh, wang2023overview, andreassen2020information}.
Disasters routinely degrade or destroy commercial telecom infrastructure precisely when coordination demand is highest~\cite{marshall2023telecommunications}, as documented in the 2018 Swedish forest-fire crisis~\cite{murphy2021digital}.
Vehicles must continue acting on stale or partial information without external coordination.
Field submissions entered on tablets must survive hours without connectivity.
In command-hierarchical environments the consequences of information loss are wrong assignments, missed hazards, and conflicting orders~\cite{allen2014information}.

The operational constraints in this paper are grounded in public Swedish rescue-service guidance: R\"addningstj\"ansten Storg\"oteborg's incident-planning and municipal rescue-plan guidance~\cite{rsg-rad201, rsg-kommunalplan}, which treats incident-planning information as an ongoing, partly secrecy-restricted process handled in operational mobile IT support rather than a static paper artifact.
Independent academic work on Swedish rescue-service information-systems practice corroborates these end-user requirements and information-flow shortcomings~\cite{pilemalm2014enabling, westman2021mobilisation}.
From these public sources we derive five constraints.
The system must operate without connection to any other tier.
Tablets need durable forward-only field submissions.
Incident-state decisions need clear command authority.
The network design must avoid cross-vehicle broadcast hazards.
The deployment must reuse existing rescue-service certificate and identity infrastructure.
Section~\ref{sec:requirements} separates these externally motivated requirements from design assumptions and integration choices.

This paper asks: \emph{how should authority-bearing data be synchronized across intermittently connected edge tiers, when the operational model designates a single principal as authoritative?}
The answer is \methodname{} (AAL); a discipline that places the incident-journal linearization point at the command edge, the vehicle edge node that currently holds operational command authority.
\systemname{} is the prototype system used to evaluate AAL.
It combines core-to-edge reference-data distribution, responder outboxes, a single incident journal, and fenced promotion.
The contribution is not a new consensus or CRDT mechanism; it is an empirical and architectural characterization of where the linearization boundary should sit for this class of rescue-service workflow.
We make three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item an empirical characterization of the authority-aligned linearization boundary, including a measured comparison with a Hanssen-style operation-based CRDT-LWW baseline on the same queue-depth workload shape and an explicitly scoped outage-duration comparison;
  \item a fenced-promotion protocol that keeps the writer of record aligned with operational command authority, specified in TLA+ and mirrored by Hypothesis state-machine tests, with a service-path check that stale-epoch traffic is rejected at the command edge;
  \item a Docker Compose and \textit{tc-netem} prototype study of responder-to-command propagation, command-to-core recovery, incident-journal linearization, idempotent replay, and the limits of the current implementation.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper motivates the requirements and architecture,
presents the synchronization protocol, describes the prototype, reports the
emulation and bounded safety results, and closes with scope and limitations.
