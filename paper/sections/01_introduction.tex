\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public-safety information systems must remain operational when commercial connectivity fails~\cite{wisniewski2023continuity, oecd2019resilience, karaman2024resilient}. Rescue-service vehicles operate up to several hours from regional dispatch centres in terrain where wireless connectivity alternates between full wide-area coverage, mesh-only operation, and total isolation~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh, wang2023overview, andreassen2020information}; disasters routinely degrade or destroy commercial telecom infrastructure precisely when coordination demand is highest~\cite{marshall2023telecommunications}, as documented in the 2018 Swedish forest-fire crisis~\cite{murphy2021digital}. Vehicles must continue acting on stale or partial information without external coordination, and field observations entered on tablets must not be lost when connectivity is unavailable for hours; in command-hierarchical environments the consequences of information loss are wrong assignments, missed hazards, and conflicting orders~\cite{allen2014information}.

A Swedish municipal rescue service, R\"addningstj\"ansten Storg\"oteborg (RSG), publishes operational guidance for incident planning and the structure of municipal rescue plans~\cite{rsg-rad201, rsg-kommunalplan}. That guidance distinguishes a generally available part of the rescue plan from a facility-specific part subject to secrecy under the Swedish Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act, and is explicit that incident-planning information is an ongoing process rather than a static paper artifact, that some plans must be handled in operational mobile IT support while others remain outside that support, and that plans contain layered information about buildings, risks, technical protection systems, and adjacent risks. Independent academic work on Swedish rescue-service IS practice has documented compatible end-user requirements and information-flow shortcomings~\cite{pilemalm2014enabling, westman2021mobilisation}. From these public sources we derive operational requirements and deployment constraints that drive the system in this paper: continued operation under disconnection from any other tier; durable forward-only submission of field observations; clear authority for incident-state decisions; predictable network behaviour without cross-vehicle broadcast hazards; and reuse of the rescue-service organisation's existing certificate and identity infrastructure rather than a parallel identity stack. Section~\ref{sec:requirements} separates these externally motivated requirements from design assumptions and integration choices.

Existing replication techniques optimize different correctness and availability properties. Consensus-based state-machine replication~\cite{lamport1998parttime, ongaro2014raft, katsarakis2020hermes} gives strong ordering when a quorum is reachable, but quorum availability may not hold during vehicle isolation or degraded incident networks. Adaptations aimed at intermittently connected emergency-response settings, such as CoNICE's hierarchical-naming consensus~\cite{jahanian2022conice}, preserve agreement semantics that still tie commits to the availability of enough participants. Multi-primary replication for disconnected operation~\cite{mucha2023database} preserves local writes but requires conflict semantics after partitions heal. CRDT-based replication~\cite{kleppmann2019localfirst, almeida2014delta, kohler2025consistent, haas2023lore} is valuable for naturally mergeable state, but authority-bearing command decisions cannot always be reduced to commutative merge semantics. The architecture in this paper chooses a narrower point in the design space: preserve one operational command writer for incident-journal state, while allowing non-command nodes to submit durable forward-only events. A recent systematic review of offline-first and edge-based emergency-response systems notes the lack of empirical comparisons between replication designs in disaster scenarios~\cite{aboualola2023edgesurvey}; our Raft measurements are included as contextual comparison points, not as workflow-equivalent benchmarks.

This paper asks: \emph{how should authority-bearing data be synchronized across intermittently connected edge tiers, when the operational model designates a single principal as authoritative?} It answers with a tiered synchronization architecture combining core-to-edge reference-data distribution, responder outboxes, a single command-side incident journal, and explicit manual promotion. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a consistency discipline for rescue-service incident response that separates authority-bearing from non-authority operations and makes the operational command the linearization point for the former;
  \item model-level evidence (TLA+ with TLC and Hypothesis state-machine tests) that a strict fenced-promotion rule preserves single-authority and no-forked-journal invariants, and that a natural weak variant violates both at shallow depth;
  \item a Docker Compose service-path prototype measuring field-edit propagation, WAN recovery, single-row commit throughput, idempotent replay, and direct command-side commit during responder isolation.
\end{enumerate}

We do not claim physical field deployment, durable service-level command epochs, automatic failover, replacement of Raft or Paxos, generic offline-first database behavior, generic mesh-platform behavior, Byzantine fault tolerance, or measured security/performance trade-offs. These are listed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} as out of scope or future work.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem-analysis} positions the design against state-machine replication, local-first systems, and edge-connectivity work, and presents the operational requirements and the three-tier architecture. Section~\ref{sec:sync} specifies the synchronization protocol. Section~\ref{sec:implementation} states implementation status. Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports the emulation results, bounded safety validation, and contextual Raft comparison.
