\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Command at an incident belongs to a person, and the organization, not the network, decides
who that person is. We separated a person's right to command from a device's right to
write, gave every order a status that the people acting on it can see, and asked what each
succession policy costs them while devices cannot reach each other and when they
reconnect. Three mechanisms decide most of that cost: a commander's device must stop by a
timeout the deputy has acknowledged, not one it measures alone, or a relayed heartbeat can
revive it; an appointment must take effect when the successor's device accepts it, or a
takeover signed from afar rewrites orders already carried out; and orders settle only once
the predecessor's records arrive or the appointing officer acts, which pre-authorized
succession has to plan for. Leases renewed at the regional server, as in our earlier
prototype, cost most in the case that needs no succession at all.
