\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}
\label{sec:validation}

\subsection{Emulation Environment}
\label{sec:eval-env}

All measurements reported in this section come from a Docker Compose
emulation that instantiates one regional core, one command-role edge node,
and three responder-role edge nodes as separate Compose projects sharing a
single Docker bridge network. The emulation does not include the Rajant mesh
radio link, the WireGuard overlay, mTLS termination at Nginx, or real-world
mobile network conditions; container-to-container traffic runs over plain
HTTP within the shared bridge network. Synthetic field events are submitted
through a Python tablet stub at configurable rates, and partitions are
injected by disconnecting and re-attaching containers from the bridge
network with the original DNS aliases preserved. Each scenario cell repeats
$N=30$ times unless otherwise noted; we report median and 95th-percentile
values. The complete record set (365 rows across six scenarios) is bundled
with the artefact in
\texttt{paper/data/eval\_metrics.jsonl}.

\subsection{Sync Protocol Latency and Recovery}
\label{sec:eval-latency}

We exercise three protocol-level scenarios: incident bootstrap from the
core, end-to-end field-edit propagation through the responder outbox to the
command journal, and recovery to the regional core after a WAN partition.
Summary statistics are in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}; the recovery
distribution is plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:recovery}.

Bootstrap latency scales sub-linearly with AOI complexity: the median rises
from 4.2 ms at 10 polygons to 10.5 ms at 1000 polygons, dominated by
polygon serialisation rather than database access. Field-edit propagation,
measuring the path from \texttt{POST /api/events} on the responder to a
committed row in \texttt{incident.journal} on the command edge, is
approximately constant at queue depth $\le 10$ (median $\approx 616$ ms)
and rises to 914 ms at queue depth 100, reflecting the 250 ms
responder-syncd poll interval compounded with batch processing on the
command side.

Recovery from a WAN partition is monotonic in partition duration. For
1\,s partitions the median recovery is 728\,ms; for 10\,s partitions
3.39\,s; for 60\,s partitions the distribution is bimodal, with a small
low-side population near 12\,s and a tight cluster at 49.7\,s. The
49.7\,s cluster reflects the per-batch HTTP timeout (10\,s) of the
long-lived \texttt{httpx.AsyncClient} used by the command-to-core
forwarder: the client retains the stale connection across the
disconnect/reconnect cycle and times out once per outbound batch on
resume. The protocol-level claim from this scenario is the monotonicity
in partition duration, not the absolute recovery number at 60\,s;
closing the connection pool on partition events would collapse the
60\,s distribution onto the single-second order of magnitude seen in
the 1\,s and 10\,s cells.

\subsection{Steady-State Throughput}
\label{sec:eval-throughput}

We saturate the command edge with concurrent submissions of 1000 events per
run and measure end-to-end commit throughput. The first run of each
saturation pass exhibits a documented warm-up artefact at $\sim$54 ev/s
(connection pool establishment, JIT optimisation); we exclude
\texttt{run\_index = 0} from the reported summary. Across the remaining 29
runs, the command edge sustains a median of 169.2 events/s, with a
95th percentile of 173.4 events/s and a maximum of 173.7 events/s. The
narrow distribution reflects per-incident serialisation through a
\texttt{FOR UPDATE} row lock on \texttt{incident.state}; under heavier loads
the lock would be the bottleneck.

\subsection{Idempotency and Safety Verification}
\label{sec:eval-safety}

The protocol's idempotency claim and the single-writer authority property
are verified by three independent methods.

\textbf{Empirical idempotency (Phase~2).} The duplicate-replay scenario
issues 50 unique \texttt{client\_event\_id} values and replays each five
times against a clean responder, then counts journal rows on the command
edge. Across $n=30$ runs no replay produced more than the 50 expected
journal rows: \texttt{stored} equals \texttt{unique\_events} in every run.

\textbf{Formal model (Phase~3).} A TLA+ specification of the protocol's
authority machinery (single-writer journal, manual promotion, partition
state) is checked with TLC under bounded parameters $|V|=3$,
$\textit{MaxJournal}=2$, $|\textit{Cids}|=2$, with full symmetry over
vehicles. The specification supports two promotion variants controlled by a
single boolean. The strict variant requires that all current commands be
reachable from the candidate vehicle before a promotion proceeds; the weak
variant allows promotion regardless. Under the strict variant, TLC explores
$386$ distinct states at depth $10$ and finds no violation of any safety
invariant. Under the weak variant, TLC produces a
\textsf{SingleAuthority} counterexample at depth~$3$ (a single partition
followed by a promotion of the unreachable side). Suppressing role-level
invariants so search continues, TLC finds a \textsf{NoForkedJournal}
violation at depth~$5$ over $922$ explored states, with a trace that lands
the textbook split-brain pattern: partition, weak promote of the unreachable
side, both sides commit at $\textit{seq}=1$ with distinct
\texttt{client\_event\_id}s. The weak counterexample is not a defect of the
adopted system; it is a confirmation that the strict precondition encoded
in the manual promotion procedure is what makes \textsf{NoForkedJournal}
hold.

\textbf{Implementation-level testing (Phase~3).} A Hypothesis state-machine
test mirrors the TLA+ model in Python and exercises the same operations
under randomly generated schedules. Across 2000 examples of length~50, no
violation of \textsf{SingleAuthority}, \textsf{NoForkedJournal}, or
\textsf{LocalIdempotency} was found under the strict variant. A separate
test reproduces the weak counterexample by direct construction, confirming
the simulator and the spec model the same property. A small smoke layer
exercises the equivalent properties against the running prototype: ten-fold
replay of a single \texttt{client\_event\_id} produces exactly one journal
row, and five distinct ids produce five.

\subsection{Comparison with Consensus-Based Replication}
\label{sec:eval-baseline}

To position the trade-off the architecture makes against a textbook
single-leader consensus baseline, we ran the same two latency scenarios
against a 3-voter \texttt{hashicorp/raft} cluster on the same Docker
Compose substrate, with the leader serving writes and quorum requiring two
voters. The full results are in
\texttt{paper/data/eval\_metrics\_raft.jsonl}; summary statistics are in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline}.

We do not present Raft as a workflow-equivalent alternative. The
responder-outbox plus manual-promotion pattern is structurally different
from leader-and-followers consensus, and a like-for-like latency
comparison would require a Raft variant with equivalent client-side
overheads --- outbox, polling interval, multi-tier forwarding --- which
we leave to future work. The comparison here isolates a single
architectural property: write availability under partition. The latency
cells in Table~\ref{tab:baseline} are reported for context, not as a
head-to-head claim.

Under no partition, the direct Raft leader path is faster than the
prototype's end-to-end field-edit path at queue depth~10 (median 10.5\,ms
versus 615.9\,ms), as expected: the baseline commits directly at the
leader and does not include the responder outbox or the responder-syncd
poll interval. After a 60\,s isolation of one voter, Raft follower
catch-up is also shorter (median 4.1\,s) than the prototype's
command-to-core WAN-recovery drain (median 49.6\,s, dominated by the
HTTP-client artefact discussed in Section~\ref{sec:eval-latency}); the
two paths measure different recovery mechanisms and are not directly
comparable on absolute time.

The architectural difference that matters for R3 appears when quorum is
lost. With two of three voters isolated, the Raft cluster cannot make
progress: all ten write attempts fail throughout the partition window.
The prototype's command vehicle is by construction unaffected by the
isolation of responder peers; it commits to its local journal regardless
of responder-fleet connectivity. This is the trade-off encoded in R3:
write availability under partition is preserved at the cost of automatic
write failover.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:eval-limits}

The measurements above are emulation results, not field measurements. We
do not exercise real Rajant mesh radio behaviour, vegetation-driven
connectivity loss reported by~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh}, sustained tablet
operation under battery and thermal constraints, or multi-incident
concurrency at the regional core. The 60 s-partition recovery distribution
is dominated by an HTTP-client artefact rather than by protocol behaviour;
the protocol-level claim is the monotonicity in partition duration, not the
absolute number. The TLA+ verification holds over a bounded state space
(3 vehicles, journal length 2, 2 client ids); the bound is small enough to
exhaust but does not by itself imply correctness at fleet scales beyond the
bound. A physical pilot in the methodology of~\cite{lindstrom2025tripod}
remains required before any field-realistic latency or survivability claim
can be made.
