\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation asks what each policy costs the people involved, and whether the mechanisms
of \cref{sec:enforcement} do what they claim. It runs on an executable model of the
scenario, which lets us cover policies the prototype does not yet implement, and thousands
of incidents per question. \Cref{sec:eval-prototype} reports what the prototype shows today
and what it must still show.

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:eval-method}

\textit{The model.} A discrete-event simulation of \cref{fig:scenario}: eight devices,
eleven links, and the people of \cref{sec:system-model}. Devices hold signed records and
exchange them by gossip over every link that is up; heartbeats carry each device's view of
the others. Every device computes who held command when and classifies every decision with
the same deterministic rule (\cref{sec:statuses}); the regional server settles decisions
(\cref{sec:settlement}). Each policy is a device-side condition and a trigger for moving
command (\cref{tab:policies}). Commanders issue decisions to random crews as a Poisson
process while their own devices say they hold command; a refused attempt is an order given
by voice only. \Cref{tab:params} lists the defaults.

\textit{Truth and metrics.} A truth oracle applies the same rule to every record ever
signed, ordered by real time. From it and from one-second probes of which devices would
accept their user's decisions, we measure: how long A keeps recording after the cut; the
takeover delay; \emph{overlap}, the time during which two people can record; \emph{no
command}, the time during which nobody can; residual decisions; valid orders refused by
their own issuer's device; how many of the predecessor's valid decisions the successor
lacked when taking over; settlement latency and decisions never settled; and
\emph{flips}, orders that a crew's device first showed as valid and later as residual,
counted once per crew device that showed them.

\textit{Cases and runs.} Seven policies, four connectivity cases (none, P-A, P-B, P-C) and
three states of A (fit and answering voice, fit but unreachable by voice, unfit), with
\tbSeedsPerCell{} seeds per cell; sweeps of $T$, $L$, the operator's reaction time, the
margin $T - T'$ and clock error; named faults; and \tbSearchRuns{} incidents with every link
flapping at random and parameters drawn at random. \tbTotalRuns{} incidents in all. Every
run is deterministic in its seed; every number quoted is a median over seeds unless stated
otherwise, and is traced to its dataset and filter. \pending{Artifact: the
\texttt{testbed/} directory of the rescue-ois repository.}

\subsection{What Each Policy Costs}
\label{sec:eval-costs}

\Cref{tab:costs} gives the main costs. Four results stand out.

\textit{A lease renewed at the server costs most when nothing is wrong with the commander.}
In P-B, A is fit and reachable over the mesh, and no succession is needed. Yet S1, S2 and
S3+L stop A after $L$ and nobody else can record: \tbPBNoCommand{} without command in a
1{,}200\,s outage, with \tbPBRefused{} valid orders refused by A's own device and given by
voice only. Under S0, S3 and S4 the same case costs nothing. The lease is the command
analogue of an emergency stop on connection loss, which is not a safe default
either~\cite{brettin2025blindspot}. This is the dominant cost of our earlier prototype's
policy, not the residual orders the conference draft expected.

\textit{S1 and S2 differ only if the operator reacts faster than $L$.} With a 120\,s
reaction and $L = 90$\,s, A has stopped before the operator promotes B, and S1 never
overlaps. It overlaps only when the operator acts within $L$ (\tbSOneOverlapFast{} with a
15\,s reaction) or $L$ exceeds the reaction time.

\textit{Without the commander's answer, takeover by C overlaps for the whole outage.} Under
S3, a fit commander who answers C's call keeps command and nothing moves; one who does not
answer is taken over after 180\,s, keeps recording for \tbSThreeSilentOverlap{} beside B, and
leaves \tbSThreeSilentResidual{} residual orders. A lease on A's side (S3+L) prevents this
only while $L$ is shorter than C's reaction: with $L = 300$\,s it overlaps for
\tbSThreeLOverlapLong{}. And when A does answer, the lease stops A while C, having heard A,
does nothing: nobody records for \tbSThreeLNoCommand{}.

\textit{Silence detects a lost device, not an unfit commander.} When A is unfit but A's
device stays connected, S4 never moves command, and nobody records for
\tbUnfitSFourNoCommand{}. S1 and S2 act on the voice report (\tbUnfitSOne{}), S3 on the report
and a failed call (\tbUnfitSThree{}). A dead-man switch that beats only while the commander
is fit lets S4 take over at $T$ (\tbUnfitSFourPerson{}), at a cost we do not measure:
takeovers when the commander is merely idle.

Under S4, with A fit and cut off, A records for \tbSFourAWindow{} and B takes over at
\tbSFourTakeover{}; nobody records for \tbSFourGap{} in between, by design. S5 overlaps for
\tbSFiveOverlap{} and leaves \tbSFiveResidual{} residual orders. In P-B with A unfit, no
policy moves command during the outage: C's takeover cannot reach the scene, and under S4
A's device is still heard over the mesh.

\subsection{Enforcement and Reconnection Faults}
\label{sec:eval-faults}

\Cref{tab:faults} tests the mechanisms of \cref{sec:enforcement} against faults.

\textit{Stale heartbeats.} In the relay witness of \cref{fig:relay}, the timeout of the
system model lets A record beside B for \tbRelayOverlap{}; the acknowledged timeout,
\tbRelayOverlapAcked{}. Across the random search, S4 overlapped in \tbFlapOverlapRuns{} of
\tbFlapRuns{} incidents with $T - T' \geq 5$\,s and no handover, and in
\tbFlapOverlapRunsAcked{} of \tbFlapRunsAcked{} with acknowledgment. In a sweep of the margin $T - T'$
with adversarial clocks, the unacknowledged rule still overlapped at \tbMarginPlainLast{}
with 5\,s heartbeats and $\epsilon = 2$\,s; the acknowledged rule showed no overlap from
\tbMarginAckedFirst{} up, for every heartbeat period and clock error tried.
\Cref{prop:single-acceptor} guarantees none once the margin exceeds $\epsilon$, whatever
the heartbeat period.

\textit{Takeovers from afar.} With A fit but silent in P-B, a takeover effective when
signed turns \tbSignedResidual{} orders residual, each of which all three crew devices had
already shown as valid (\tbSignedFlips{} flips). Effective on acceptance, \tbAcceptedResidual{} orders made during the outage
turn residual, with \tbAcceptedOverlap{} of overlap at the heal while A learns of the change.

\textit{One-way links and handovers inside a partition.} The system model expected a link
that works in one direction to leave both A and B accepting. It does not: if A's device can
receive but not send, A hears of B's activation and the overlap lasts \tbMuteOverlap{}; if it
can send but not receive, A stops at $T'$, B never activates, and nobody records for
\tbDeafNoCommand{}. A handover by A to S inside a P-C partition, while B activates in the
other part, makes B's whole tenure residual under the precedence rule (\tbHandoverResidual{}
orders, \tbHandoverOverlap{} of S and B both recording): two successions in two partitions
are the plural case in disguise, and any deterministic precedence turns one side's orders
residual. \pending{Precedence between a handover and an activation: decision for the
supervisors.}

\subsection{Settlement}
\label{sec:eval-settlement}

With A incapacitated in P-A and A's device lost for good, the system model's settlement
rule, read literally, leaves \tbStrictLostNeverMin{} to \tbStrictLostNeverMax{} valid
decisions per incident never settled (medians by policy): every decision after A's last
heartbeat. With the rule of \cref{prop:settlement}, S1 and S3 settle all decisions but at most A's
last, within \tbClosingSettlePninetyfive{} (95th percentile). Under S4 the deputy's
decisions wait for A's device: \tbSFourSettlePninetyfiveKept{} at the 95th percentile when it
reconnects at the heal, and \tbSFourNeverSettledLost{} decisions never settled when it is
lost. In every policy and partition case, decisions made during a partition settle only
after the heal, so crews and operators work from provisional orders for the length of the
outage. \pending{Whether that is workable: ask the rescue service.}

\subsection{Invariants under Random Flapping}
\label{sec:eval-search}

Across the \tbSearchRuns{} random incidents, under both settlement rules and with the
acknowledged and acceptance variants: every device ended with the same records and no
accepted decision was lost. Authoritative verdicts later revoked by some device:
\tbSearchRevoked{}. Final verdicts that disagreed with the truth oracle while clock error
stayed within $\epsilon$: \tbSearchMisclassified{}. The search found two faults
in our own first implementation of acceptance---settlement that ignored a pending appointee,
and a validity check that compared two devices' clocks---both fixed before the runs reported
here. It is evidence, not proof; \pending{TLA+ for \cref{prop:single-acceptor} and part (i)
of \cref{prop:settlement}.}

Clock error matters little at GNSS accuracy: with $\epsilon$ up to 2\,s, no decision was in
doubt at one decision per 20\,s; at 10\,s and 60\,s, \tbDoubtTen{} and \tbDoubtSixty{} per
incident. A clock beyond the bound misclassifies decisions near a change of command, as
expected.

\subsection{Choosing the Parameters}
\label{sec:eval-parameters}

Under S4, a takeover while A is fit happens exactly when the outage outlasts $T$: the rate
of needless takeovers is the probability that a commander's data outage lasts longer than
$T$, a distribution only the rescue service can supply. Waiting longer costs completeness:
when B takes over, B lacks \tbBlindSixty{}, \tbBlindThreeHundred{} and \tbBlindTwelveHundred{}
of A's valid decisions at $T = 60$, 300 and 1{,}200\,s. For the lease policies, $L$ trades the
time without command in P-B ($D - L$ for an outage of length $D$) against overlap when $L$
exceeds the time the operator or C takes to act. \pending{Outage-length distribution and
reaction times: ask the rescue service; then report expected costs per policy.}

\subsection{The Prototype}
\label{sec:eval-prototype}

The prototype, built before the authority model, implements S1 on Python~3.12 services
with FastAPI and asyncpg, run under Docker Compose with one regional core, two vehicle
edges that can each hold command, and responder edges. The core issues each command tenure
an epoch, accepts journal entries only from the edge recorded as holding the current epoch,
and renews the holder's lease on traffic and heartbeats; an operator promotes another edge
after copying the core's journal prefix. Across two independent vehicle edges under a real
partition, a superseded edge that reconnects is refused with HTTP~409 and demotes itself,
and a holder that loses only its backhaul stops after its lease ($L = 15$\,s) and resumes
when the link returns: the cost of \cref{sec:eval-costs} in miniature. Stale-epoch writes are
refused in a median of \promotionMedianB{}, before any journal write, against
\promotionMedianA{} for a commit. A TLA+ model of the strict promotion protocol passes six
safety invariants over 38.56\,M states, and a three-voter Raft cluster moves leadership to
the reachable majority in all twelve runs: consensus cannot carry the organization's
decision. \pending{Condense further once the extension below exists.}

The prototype falls short of the model: it binds authority to a vehicle, not a person; it
has no grants, signed decisions or succession acts; promotion needs the core; and it does
not label residual decisions. \pending{Planned: persons, signed grants and succession
acts; the anchor policy with appointments effective on acceptance and settlement; runs on
the multi-host testbed for the reconnection cases of \cref{tab:faults}, compared with the
model's predictions for the same link schedules.}
