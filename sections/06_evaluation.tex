\subsection{Authority boundary and journal sequencing (M1--M2)}
\label{sec:eval-independence}
\label{sec:eval-m1}
\label{sec:eval-m2}

M1--M2 serve R3, and R1 for command-side writes.

One responder is isolated for ten seconds while single-event POSTs go directly
to \textit{/accept/event-batch}. Were responder reachability in the command
commit path, these would stall; they do not (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}), and
stale or absent \textit{X-Command-Epoch} values are rejected before any journal
work.

Saturation throughput at the linearization point bounds what a single writer can
absorb. A generous 10 authority-bearing events per minute is 0.17 events/s,
three orders of magnitude below the measured rate (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}).

\subsection{Durable outbox and idempotent submission (M3)}
\label{sec:eval-m3}

M3 (the durable responder outbox with \textit{client\_event\_id} idempotency)
serves R1 and R2.

Propagation is measured across queue depths 1--100, the range a responder
accumulates between contacts (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}).

Two properties are not tabulated. Replaying 50 unique
\textit{client\_event\_id} values five times each leaves exactly 50 journal rows
across $n=30$ runs, so at-least-once forwarding is exactly-once at the
linearization point. And 50 outbox submissions survive a responder-Postgres
power cycle, forwarding afterwards with no duplicates and consecutive
\textit{event\_seq}, journal-consistent about 2.6\,s after recovery---the
runtime witness for R2.

\subsection{Fenced promotion (M4)}
\label{sec:eval-promotion}
\label{sec:eval-m4}

M4 (fenced promotion with stale-epoch rejection) serves R3 under command
transfer and exercises C2.

A promotion is driven end to end with every write attempt timed by phase:
commits at epoch~$e$, stale-epoch rejections at the boundary, then commits at
epoch~$e+1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:promotion-cost}). Fencing costs single-digit
milliseconds on the service path, and the rejection path is cheaper than the
commit path by construction, because the fence refuses stale traffic before the
journal write. Operator confirmation sits in the critical
path by design and dominates wall-clock, so the protocol contribution is an
operator cost rather than a network cost.

Across five runs the fence holds exactly: 20/20 commits at epoch~1, 20/20
HTTP~409 rejections adding zero journal rows, 20/20 commits at epoch~2, and a
200-row journal split 100/100 between epochs with no \textit{event\_seq} value
under both.

\subsubsection{Two-edge fenced promotion}
\label{sec:eval-two-edge}

The results above hold at one acceptor. Promotion is additionally exercised
across two independent vehicle edges, each with its own database, with the
incumbent partitioned from the network: the candidate bootstraps the journal
prefix from core, verifies it is contiguous, obtains the next epoch, and only
then takes the role. This is the case R3 actually has to survive, and both
outcomes are measured rather than asserted.

When the superseded edge reconnects and forwards what it buffered while
isolated, core refuses it because another node holds the epoch; the edge demotes
itself, and its own accept path then refuses authority-bearing writes. Its
buffered rows are retained for reconciliation and the authoritative journal is
unchanged throughout---the fork is contained at the journal boundary and never
admitted. When instead the \emph{current} holder loses only its backhaul, it
accepts writes while its lease is valid and refuses them once the lease lapses,
recovering when the link returns. It is never informed that anything happened;
it stops on a timeout it cannot renew, which is the honest form of the guarantee
and the reason the availability cost falls where it does.

We report no fencing latency: the transition is dominated by the lease window,
an operator-chosen parameter rather than a property of the protocol.

Verification and falsification costs are strongly asymmetric. The strict
variant passes all six safety invariants over 38.56\,M distinct states in
13.0\,min, whereas each weak variant is refuted in under a second:
\textit{SingleAuthority} in 185 states, \textit{EpochAuthorityCoupling} in 144,
and \textit{NoForkedJournal} in 1.0\,k. Establishing the property is expensive;
refuting the weakened rule is not.

\subsubsection{CRDT semantics fail for authority-bearing data}
\label{sec:eval-crdt}

Four FastAPI replicas run Hanssen's operation-based Last-Writer-Wins Element
Set~\cite{hanssen2025emergency} against constructed authority-bearing conflicts
(Table~\ref{tab:crdt-comparison}). This tests C1---whether mergeable-by-default
replication can encode authority for non-commutative incident decisions---and is
a semantic falsification, not a speed comparison: LWW discards the losing status
from materialized state, whereas \systemname{} preserves both writes in an
attributed total order. Ordering and attribution are R3's service-path scope;
semantic rejection is an additional guard disclosed in
\S~\ref{sec:non-claims}.

The baseline is deliberately lightweight and in-memory, converging only through
harness-triggered \textit{full\_sync()}. That asymmetry favors CRDT where it
already performs better, which is the correct bias for a falsification: if
mergeable-by-default semantics cannot encode authority even under favorable
conditions, the argument holds regardless of infrastructure differences.

\subsection{Time-varying connectivity and fleet size}
\label{sec:eval-network}

The cells above hold connectivity fixed and use a single responder. Neither
matches a rescue deployment: vehicle connectivity alternates between regimes,
and incidents involve several responders at once. Each axis is varied in turn.

\subsubsection{Regime transitions, not steady state}
\label{sec:eval-schedule}

A single continuous submission stream runs while the responder-to-command path
walks a schedule of regimes---clear, mesh-stressed, mesh-degraded, isolated,
clear---with every event attributed to the regime in force when it was submitted
(Table~\ref{tab:netem-schedule}). Impairment is destination-filtered, so only
the peer path is affected and each edge keeps full-speed access to its own
database. This observes the durable-outbox property \emph{across} a transition
rather than inferring it from steady state, and delivery within the isolated
phase is zero: that is what a single-writer boundary costs while the path to the
writer is down.

No event is lost. All 540 reach the journal, all ten runs drain fully, and the
outbox backlog peaks at 13 events. Recovery is sub-second in every run, measured
from the instant connectivity is restored until every event pending at that
instant is journal-visible. Measurement resolution is about 0.4~s, set by the
polling interval and query cost. Recovery time is bounded by the forwarder's
poll cycle and backlog depth, not by how long the outage lasted.

\subsubsection{Fleet size}
\label{sec:eval-fleet}

Fleet sizes of 1, 3, 5 and 10 independent responder edges---each with its own
database and outbox---submit simultaneously, five runs each, 20 events per
responder (Table~\ref{tab:fleet-scaling}).
\S~\ref{sec:eval-m1} argues that a single-writer serializer is adequate because
its saturation rate exceeds plausible incident demand; that argument is only as
good as its behaviour when responders multiply. It holds: the linearization
point absorbs a ten-edge fleet without becoming the bottleneck.

Two limits are worth stating. These throughput figures are not comparable to the
saturation rate in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}---a 200-event batch is dominated
by fixed per-event latency rather than serializer capacity, so both are lower
bounds on different axes. And ten edges is where this single host runs out of
memory, not where \systemname{} runs out of headroom: 73 containers share one
kernel, one disk and one bridge. The sweep establishes the shape of the curve,
not an absolute ceiling.
