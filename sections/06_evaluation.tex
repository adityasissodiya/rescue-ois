\subsection{Authority boundary and journal sequencing (M1--M2)}
\label{sec:eval-independence}
\label{sec:eval-m1}
\label{sec:eval-m2}
\label{sec:eval-fleet}

M1--M2 serve R3, and R1 for command-side writes.

One responder is isolated for ten seconds while single-event POSTs go directly
to \textit{/accept/event-batch}. Were responder reachability in the command
commit path, these would stall; they do not (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}), and
stale or absent \textit{X-Command-Epoch} values are rejected before any journal
work.

Saturation throughput at the sequencer bounds what a single writer can
absorb. A generous 10 decisions per minute is 0.17 events/s,
three orders of magnitude below the measured rate (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}).

\textit{Fleet size.} That argument is only as good as the sequencer's behaviour when
responders multiply. Fleets of 1, 3, 5 and 10 independent responder edges, each with its
own database and outbox, submit simultaneously, five runs each and 20 events per
responder (Figure~\ref{fig:fleet-scaling}). The sequencer absorbs a ten-edge fleet
without becoming the bottleneck. These rates are lower bounds on a different axis from
the saturation rate in Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}, because a 200-event batch is dominated
by fixed per-event latency. Ten edges is where this single host runs out of memory (73
containers), not where \systemname{} runs out of headroom.

\subsection{Durable outbox and idempotent submission (M3)}
\label{sec:eval-m3}
\label{sec:eval-schedule}

M3 (the durable responder outbox with \textit{client\_event\_id} idempotency)
serves R1 and R2.

Propagation is measured across queue depths 1--100, the range a responder
accumulates between contacts (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}).

Two properties are not tabulated. Replaying 50 unique
\textit{client\_event\_id} values five times each leaves exactly 50 journal rows
across $n=30$ runs, so at-least-once forwarding is exactly-once at the
sequencer. And 50 outbox submissions survive a responder-Postgres
power cycle, forwarding afterwards with no duplicates and consecutive
\textit{event\_seq}, journal-consistent about 2.6\,s after recovery---the
runtime witness for R2.

\textit{Connectivity regimes.} The cells above hold connectivity fixed. A single
continuous submission stream therefore also runs while the responder-to-command path
walks a schedule of connectivity regimes, each edge keeping full-speed access to its own
database (Figure~\ref{fig:netem-schedule}). The backlog rises only while the path to the
writer is down: local acceptance continues, ordering waits, and nothing is lost anywhere
in the schedule.

\subsection{Fenced promotion (M4)}
\label{sec:eval-promotion}
\label{sec:eval-m4}

M4 serves R3 under command transfer and exercises C2.

\textit{Fencing cost at one acceptor.} A promotion is driven end to end with every
write attempt timed, \promotionNPerPhase{} attempts per phase. Commits before and after
the transition take a median of \promotionMedianA{} and \promotionMedianC{}, and
stale-epoch attempts are refused in a median of \promotionMedianB{}, before any journal
write. Across five runs the fence holds exactly: 20/20 commits at epoch~1, 20/20
HTTP~409 rejections adding zero journal rows, 20/20 commits at epoch~2, and a 200-row
journal split 100/100 between epochs with no \textit{event\_seq} value under both.
Operator confirmation dominates wall-clock time, so a promotion costs operator time,
not network time.

\subsubsection{Two-edge fenced promotion}
\label{sec:eval-two-edge}

The single-acceptor result says nothing about a cut-off command edge, the case R3 has
to survive. Promotion is therefore also run across two independent vehicle edges, each
with its own database, with the incumbent partitioned from the network as in
Figure~\ref{fig:promotion-timeline}. Two outcomes were measured.

\emph{A superseded edge is told.} When A reconnects, the core refuses its batch with
HTTP~409 (\textit{not\_epoch\_holder}); A demotes itself, and its own accept path then
refuses writes. The core's journal is unchanged, and the rows A accepted while cut off
stay on A for review.

\emph{A holder that loses only its backhaul is told nothing.} B, the legitimate holder,
accepts decisions while its lease is valid (here $L=15$\,s), refuses them once the lease
lapses, and accepts again after the link returns and it renews. This is the lease's
cost: the fence cannot tell a superseded edge from a disconnected one.

We report no fencing latency, because the transition is dominated by $L$, a
deployment parameter rather than a property of the protocol.

Verification and falsification costs are strongly asymmetric. The strict variant passes
all six safety invariants over 38.56\,M distinct states in 13.0\,min, whereas each weak
variant is refuted in under a second: \textit{SingleAuthority} in 185 states,
\textit{EpochAuthorityCoupling} in 144, and \textit{NoForkedJournal} in 1.0\,k. Both
models cover handover from a reachable command edge and have no lease, so the cut-off
case above has a runtime witness only (\S~\ref{sec:non-claims}).

\subsection{Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:eval-scope}

\subsubsection{Raft leadership follows the reachable quorum}
\label{sec:eval-raft}

Each run hard-cuts the current leader $L_0$ of a three-voter
\textit{hashicorp/raft} cluster from the other two voters, after a sanity check
that its FSM assigns \textit{event\_seq} and enforces \textit{client\_event\_id}
idempotency. All twelve runs elect an $L_1 \ne L_0$ and commit five writes there
(median 3.08\,s, p95 4.43\,s). Isolated, $L_0$ stalls and fails with an apply
timeout during the election (median 269.9\,ms) and refuses immediately once it
has stepped down (0.4\,ms); reporting only the first would be cherry-picking its
worst phase. Both are correct Raft behavior~\cite{ongaro2014raft} and both are
the mismatch R3 names: authority follows the reachable quorum, and no input
exists by which an operator can pin the writer to a designated command edge.

\subsubsection{CRDT merge cannot designate the writer}
\label{sec:eval-crdt}

Four FastAPI replicas run Hanssen's operation-based Last-Writer-Wins (LWW) Element
Set~\cite{hanssen2025emergency} against constructed conflicting decisions
(Table~\ref{tab:crdt-comparison}). This tests merge as the authority mechanism, not
CRDTs as replication (\S~\ref{sec:positioning}), and it is a semantic falsification,
not a speed comparison. LWW discards the losing status from materialized state, whereas
\systemname{} keeps both writes in an attributed total order. Ordering and attribution
are R3's scope; semantic rejection of conflicting values is an additional guard that is
not implemented (\S~\ref{sec:non-claims}).

The baseline is deliberately lightweight and in-memory and converges only through
harness-triggered \textit{full\_sync()}. This favors CRDT, the correct bias for a
falsification: if merge cannot designate the writer under favorable conditions, it
cannot under realistic ones.

\subsubsection{Backhaul recovers within about a second of WAN heal}
\label{sec:eval-wan}

A narrowly targeted \textit{tc} cut of the command-to-core path only isolates
backhaul flush time after a WAN heal (Table~\ref{tab:evaluation}). Recovery is
bounded by the forward poll loop and backlog rather than by outage length, so
the non-monotonic medians across 1, 10 and 60~s outages are expected poll-phase
effects.

Every cell leaves the core's journal single-writer: no entry is admitted from a node
that does not hold the epoch, and no field submission is lost.
\S~\ref{sec:non-claims} lists what the evaluation does not show.
