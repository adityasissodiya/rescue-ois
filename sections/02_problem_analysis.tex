\section{System Model and Problem}
\label{sec:problem-analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:setting} shows the target setting, municipal rescue-service incident
response; the regional core also serves dispatch and reference data.

\subsection{Operational Requirements}
\label{sec:requirements}

R1 and R2 follow from mobile-IT and incident-reporting findings that responders need
locally available information and durable later
documentation~\cite{rsg-kommunalplan,pilemalm2014enabling,westman2021mobilisation}; R3
follows from decision-provenance requirements and rescue-service command
guidance~\cite{naja2010provenance,rsg-rad201}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\textit{R1 (Offline operation)}} Each tier must keep working from
  previously synchronized data without continuous connectivity.
  \item \textbf{\textit{R2 (Durable forward-only submission)}} Field submissions entered
  on tablets must survive vehicle restart and prolonged partition.
  \item \textbf{\textit{R3 (Attributable command under succession)}} Each decision must be
  attributable to an authenticated person and to the grant under which that person made
  it. For each decision, the system must determine whether the grant was valid under the
  organization's succession rule when the decision was made, and it must keep every
  decision for which it was not.
\end{itemize}

R3 replaces an earlier, stronger requirement that all decisions fall into one order under
one authority. That requirement cannot be met while the commander is cut off
(\S~\ref{sec:tradeoff}), and our prototype did not meet it: it guaranteed only that the
core admits decisions from one epoch at a time. The new R3 asks for what a system can
always provide---attribution, a verdict on validity, and no silent loss---and leaves to the
succession policy how often a decision ends up on the wrong side.

R1 and R2 need replication only: each node works from its own copy, and field
submissions are stored locally and forwarded later. Reference data, field submissions,
and materialized views can therefore use weaker, eventual contracts
(\S~\ref{sec:state-taxonomy}). R3 concerns decisions alone.

\subsection{System Model}
\label{sec:system-model}

\pending{Not yet agreed with the supervisors. The role of the superior officer C
in Swedish practice, and whether pre-authorized succession exists, are still to be checked
with the rescue service.}

\textit{People and roles.} A primary commander A, a deputy B, and a superior officer C who
appoints commanders and may take over command. The organization has one commander at a
time. Shared or contested authority, where several people must agree, is out of scope:
it needs agreement rather than designation (\S~\ref{sec:thesis}).

\textit{Grants.} C issues A a signed role grant. A succession rule, such as ``B may assume
command if A has been silent for $T$'', is signed by C in advance. An explicit handover
from A to B, and a takeover by C, are signed events. Every grant and succession event is
itself recorded.

\textit{Decisions.} A decision is signed by the person who makes it and carries the grant
it is made under, a per-person sequence number, and GNSS time.

\textit{Nodes.} Vehicle edges (command or responder), a regional core, and responder
tablets. A node exercises the authority of the person using it. It holds none of its own,
so a commander who changes vehicles takes their authority along.

\textit{Channels.} Data backhaul from vehicles to the core (cellular), a local mesh
between vehicles at the scene, and voice radio (Rakel). Voice is outside the system, but
it is how succession is usually agreed; the system records the outcome.

\textit{Partition cases.} \emph{P-A}: only A's edge is cut off. \emph{P-B}: every edge
loses backhaul at once, while the local mesh still works. \emph{P-C}: the local mesh also
splits, so A and B are in different parts.

\textit{Journal and residuals.} The \emph{authoritative journal} holds the decisions
admitted under a grant that was valid, according to the succession rule, when they were
made. A decision binds when it is admitted. A \emph{residual} decision was accepted at an
edge under a grant that had already been superseded. Residuals are kept, attributed and
labelled; they are never merged into the journal and never dropped. Deciding what to do
about a residual is an organizational act outside this paper (\S~\ref{sec:thesis}).

\textit{Assumptions.} Messages may be delayed, duplicated and reordered, and are
eventually resent. Clock drift is bounded by GNSS when it is available. Nodes are not
Byzantine, and credentials are not stolen.

\subsection{The Authority Trade-off under Partition}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

Let A's edge lose contact with every other node. It can receive no message, including one
saying that command has passed to someone else. While A is cut off, a design can provide
at most two of the following:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item A keeps recording decisions;
  \item command moves to B without A's participation;
  \item at most one disconnected edge accepts decisions at any moment.
\end{enumerate}
With (a) and (b), A and B both record. With (b) and (c), A must stop before B starts;
since A cannot be told, it must stop on a local timer, which bounds (a). With (a) and
(c), B must wait for A. This is the availability--consistency tension of partitioned
networks~\cite{davidson1985consistency,gilbert2002cap}, applied to the authority role rather
than to the data. ``No two commanders at once'' is the organization's rule, and we assume
it; at disconnected edges a system can guarantee it only by giving up (a) or (b).
Primary/backup systems escape the trade-off with an out-of-band fence that powers the old
primary off~\cite{kleppmann2017ddia}; a vehicle out of radio range offers no such channel.

Two distinctions keep the consequences apart. A \emph{legitimacy} gap is a decision
accepted under a superseded grant: a residual. A \emph{completeness} gap is a valid
decision that the journal does not yet hold, because A made it before the cut and has not
reconnected. A policy that stops A early avoids residuals but not completeness gaps.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:positioning}

The machinery is established; what this paper adds is who may confer authority and what
happens to decisions on the wrong side of a succession.

\textit{Leases, locks and fencing.} Leases bound how long a holder may act without
contact~\cite{gray1989leases}, and Paxos-based services lease mastership so that the
elected master can serve reads alone for a bounded time~\cite{chandra2007paxoslive}. Lock
services such as Chubby~\cite{burrows2006chubby} and
ZooKeeper~\cite{hunt2010zookeeper} grant leases from a replicated coordinator, and fencing
tokens let a resource refuse a holder whose lease has lapsed~\cite{kleppmann2017ddia}.
All of them assume the coordinator, not the organization, decides who holds the lease, and
that the resource can reach the coordinator. Our lease is the same mechanism, but its
length is an organizational choice: how long a cut-off commander may go on recording.

\textit{Leaders, views and reconfiguration.} Consensus and replicated state machines
provide totally ordered logs under their failure
models~\cite{lamport1998parttime,ongaro2014raft,balakrishnan2013tango,vanrenesse2004chain}.
Viewstamped Replication~\cite{oki1988viewstamped} and Zab~\cite{junqueira2011zab} order
the commands of successive leaders by view or epoch, which is the role epochs play here.
In all of them the reachable quorum chooses the leader, so a commander on the minority
side cannot commit (\S~\ref{sec:eval-raft}). Vertical Paxos~\cite{lamport2009vertical} is
closer: an external master reconfigures the replicas. Our superior officer C plays that
role, except that C is a person who is often unreachable, and the configuration change is
a signed grant rather than a protocol message.

\textit{When agreement is needed.} Brewer recasts the partition trade-off as managing
partitions explicitly: detect them, limit what runs during them, and recover
afterwards~\cite{brewer2012cap}. Invariant confluence identifies the operations that can
run without coordination while preserving an application's
invariants~\cite{bailis2014coordination}, the CALM theorem ties coordination-free
consistency to monotone programs~\cite{hellerstein2020calm}, and RedBlue consistency
coordinates only the operations that need it~\cite{li2012redblue}. \pending{Depends on the
system model under review: judging a decision by the grant it was made under is not
monotone, because a succession record that arrives late can turn a valid decision
residual. The model therefore keeps such a decision provisional until the appointing
authority confirms that the records are complete, instead of having devices agree.}

\textit{Delegation and access control under weak consistency.} Signed authorization
certificates and delegation chains, such as SPKI~\cite{rfc2693spki}, can be checked
offline, and Sudarsan et al.\ survey delegated and self-contained authorization for
cyber-physical systems and the IoT~\cite{sudarsan2021survey}. That is what lets a deputy's
edge verify a succession rule without the core in case P-B. Closer to our problem is
access control over weakly consistent replicas. Wobber et al.\ keep replicated data
eventually consistent even though policy, and with it the verdict on an operation, can
differ between nodes for a while~\cite{wobber2010policy}. Yanakieva et al.\ show that
confidentiality, integrity and accessibility cannot all hold in a highly available,
partition-tolerant file system~\cite{yanakieva2021access}, a trade-off of the same shape
as ours. Jacob and Hartenstein separate a partial order of storage, in which the past is
final, from a total order of execution, in which the past is a belief that can
change~\cite{jacob2025eventually}. Tamper-evident logs keep such a record trustworthy
afterwards~\cite{schneier1999audit}. We use these mechanisms as given; the question here
is what the grant should say and when it takes effect. \pending{Depends on the system
model under review: provisional and residual decisions are Jacob and Hartenstein's
distinction applied to command authority.}

\textit{CRDTs and local-first systems.} Mergeable replicated
state~\cite{almeida2014delta,hanssen2025emergency,almeida2024crdtsurvey,mao2024reliablecrdt}
and local-first software~\cite{kleppmann2019localfirst} serve R1 and R2 well, but as an
authority mechanism a multi-writer CRDT lets every replica decide, and its merge rule
picks a winner between conflicting decisions. This rules out merge as the source of
authority, not CRDTs as replication: once decisions are signed and attributed, a
grow-only set keyed by person and sequence number replicates them without conflict.

\textit{Emergency and edge networking.} CoNICE~\cite{jahanian2022conice} reaches
consensus among infrastructure-less peers; its no-privileged-peer assumption is exactly
the one incident command removes. Public-safety and edge research places resilient
transport and storage near
responders~\cite{kumbhar2015legacy,wang2023overview,sagor2024distressnetng}, which serves R1
but not the question of who may issue a decision.

\textit{Incident command and tactical networks.} Incident command systems combine
hierarchical and network forms~\cite{moynihan2008combining}: they coordinate many response
organizations under a temporary hierarchy, and the network character of a response shapes
coordination, authority and trust within it~\cite{moynihan2009network}. We model only the
designated part, one commander at a time changed by explicit or pre-agreed acts, and
leave shared authority to the plural pole. Military command and control faces the same
disconnected, intermittent and limited links, and communications middleware for tactical
networks is built to tolerate them~\cite{suri2009tactical}; it does not decide who may
issue an order. \pending{Still to cover: how Swedish rescue services transfer command in
practice, once the rescue service has been asked.}

\section{Succession Policies}
\label{sec:policies}

Since no design escapes the trade-off, succession is a policy choice.
Table~\ref{tab:policies} lists six policies. The entries are hypotheses derived from the
model, not measurements; the evaluation is meant to test them (\S~\ref{sec:eval-policies}).

Three observations follow from the model. First, under S2 and S4, the decisions A made
legitimately before the cut are missing from the journal until A reconnects: a
completeness gap, not a legitimacy gap, and the metrics must keep the two apart. Second,
in case P-B no edge reaches the core, so S1 and S2, which promote through the core,
cannot move command at all. S3 and S4 can, provided B's edge can verify the signed
succession event or rule locally; the place where decisions are admitted then moves from
the core to whoever holds the grant. \pending{How the model represents that move is open.}
Third, the lease stops being an arbitrary timer. Under S2 it is how long A may record
before command can move; under S4, $T$ and $T'$ are the organization's choice of how long
to wait for a silent commander, which is why we ask the rescue service what $T$ should be.

\textit{Where each policy fails.} Every policy fails in a stated case. Under S4 in P-C,
an A that ignores its own timeout keeps recording beside B, and all its later decisions
become residuals. Any policy fails with stolen credentials, which the model excludes. And
none applies to shared or contested authority, which needs agreement rather than
designation.
