\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public-safety information systems must remain operational when commercial connectivity fails.
Rescue-service vehicles operate up to several hours away from regional dispatch centres. In that terrain, wireless connectivity alternates between full wide-area coverage, mesh-only operation, and total isolation~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh, wang2023overview}.
Disasters routinely degrade or destroy commercial telecom infrastructure precisely when
coordination demand is highest. Vehicles must continue acting on stale or partial
information, and field submissions entered on tablets must survive hours without
connectivity. In command-hierarchical environments the consequences of information loss
are wrong assignments, missed hazards, and conflicting orders.

The operational constraints are grounded in public Swedish rescue-service
guidance~\cite{rsg-rad201, rsg-kommunalplan}, which treats incident-planning information
as an ongoing, secrecy-restricted process rather than a static artifact; independent
studies of Swedish rescue-service information systems confirm these requirements and the
gaps in information flow~\cite{pilemalm2014enabling, westman2021mobilisation}.
From these public sources we derive three requirements and two deployment
constraints. The requirements are \textit{R1:} offline operation, \textit{R2:} durable forward-only
field submission, and \textit{R3:} attributable command authority. The deployment
constraints are avoiding cross-vehicle broadcast hazards and reusing existing
rescue-service certificate and identity infrastructure.

The design question is: \emph{how should authority-bearing data be synchronized across intermittently connected edge tiers, when the operational model designates a single principal as authoritative?}
The answer is \methodname{} (\systemname{}), a design discipline that places the
incident-journal sequencing point at the command edge, the vehicle edge node currently
holding operational command authority. We specify and verify \systemname{} using
TLA+~\cite{tlaplus} and Hypothesis~\cite{maciver2019hypothesis} and implement it in a
prototype built around responder outboxes, a single incident journal, and fenced
promotion: the operator-initiated transfer of command authority between edges.

\systemname{} is a newly named design discipline, not a newly invented
primitive. Single-writer replication, transactional outboxes, idempotency keys,
epochs, and fencing are all established; what is new is the domain-derived
authority boundary that decides which mechanism governs which incident data.
We make three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item (C1) a problem analysis of authority-bearing incident coordination under intermittent vehicle-edge connectivity, and an authority/state-class boundary that neither local-first, CRDT-based, nor consensus-based designs directly satisfy, formalized in TLA+ and mirrored by two independent Hypothesis state machines;

  \item (C2) a two-edge realization of fenced promotion---core-issued per-incident command epochs, journal-prefix bootstrap as a promotion precondition, epoch-holder binding at the journal boundary, and a renewable authority lease---implemented and measured across two independent vehicle-edge instances under a real network partition, including the case where the superseded edge cannot be informed at all;

  \item (C3) a partition-based evaluation on an executable prototype\footnote{The prototype repository is provided as an artifact with this submission.} (Docker Compose, \textit{tc-netem}) against Hanssen-style CRDT-LWW and Raft baselines, separating safety and authority claims from availability and latency trade-offs, and extending beyond static impairment to transitions between connectivity regimes and to fleets of up to ten concurrent responder edges.
\end{enumerate}
