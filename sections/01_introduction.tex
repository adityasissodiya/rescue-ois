\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A rescue service fighting a forest fire is a cyber-physical system in which people close
the loop. Crews, vehicles and equipment act on the ground; an incident commander decides
where they go; and the commander's orders travel to them through vehicle computers,
handhelds and radio links that come and go with the terrain. Wireless connectivity at such
an incident alternates between wide-area coverage, a mesh between vehicles, and
isolation~\cite{zimbelman2022mesh,wang2023overview}, and Swedish rescue-service guidance
treats the incident's information as an ongoing, secrecy-restricted
record~\cite{rsg-rad201,rsg-kommunalplan,pilemalm2014enabling,westman2021mobilisation}.

Command belongs to a \emph{person}, not to a device. Every resource assignment, status
transition and hazard annotation---a \emph{decision}, or order---carries the authority of
the person who made it. When the commander is cut off, the organization may decide that
command passes to a deputy or to a superior officer, usually agreed over voice radio. The
devices of the people involved may not be able to reach one another when this happens,
and crews keep acting on whatever orders their devices show. When the links return, the
system holds orders from two people and must say which of them stand.

Ordering is not the hard part. One person cannot be in two places at once, so a per-person
sequence number and GNSS time order that person's decisions. The hard part is the
cut-over between two people's authority, and what it does to the people who acted on the
orders. Sorting by time yields a tidy record of conflicting orders, such as ``crew~2 to the
north flank'' from the cut-off commander and ``crew~2 to the village edge'' from the
deputy, without saying which one was valid, and without telling crew~2 which order it
should have followed.

Two bodies of work come close. Research on human-in-the-loop cyber-physical systems
transfers control authority between a human and automation, over an actuator, with graded
shared control while the link works, interlocked handover protocols that confirm the
receiving side is in the loop, and local fallback when the link is
lost~\cite{flemisch2012balance,abbink2018topology,gil2019designing,darvish2023teleoperation,reyesmunoz2023rpas}.
It has little to say about transfer between human operators under degraded links, and
almost nothing about what happens to authority and decisions when the links
return (\cref{sec:related-hitl}). Distributed systems provide the mechanisms---leases,
fencing tokens, epochs, signed delegation---but let something other than the organization
choose who may decide: consensus elects whichever node the reachable quorum
chooses~\cite{ongaro2014raft}, a multi-writer replicated data type lets its merge rule
pick between conflicting decisions~\cite{hanssen2025emergency}, and access control over
weakly consistent replicas keeps verdicts revisable without saying when they become
final~\cite{wobber2010policy,jacob2025eventually}.

We separate a person's \emph{right to command} from a device's \emph{right to write}.
Authority comes from signed role grants and from succession rules the organization fixes
in advance; devices enforce it but hold none of their own. While a commander is cut off, no
design can let the commander keep recording, move command without the commander, and keep
one device at a time accepting orders, all at once (\cref{sec:tradeoff}). Succession is
therefore a choice of policy, and the organization should be able to see what each choice
costs. This leads to our research question:

\begin{quote}
\itshape How can an intermittently connected incident-response system represent the
transfer of command authority between people, enforce it on devices that cannot reach each
other, and reconcile it when they reconnect, and what does each succession policy cost the
people who act on its decisions?
\end{quote}

The mechanisms we use---signed records, leases, epochs, completeness proofs---are
established~\cite{gray1989leases,kleppmann2017ddia,lampson1992authentication}. The
contribution is how they must be shaped when authority belongs to people, and what that
costs:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(C1)] \textbf{An authority model for human command through devices}
  (\cref{sec:model}). Authority belongs to people through signed grants, succession acts
  and rules; devices enforce it; every order carries one of four statuses
  (authoritative, provisional, residual, in doubt) that the people who act on it can see.
  \item[(C2)] \textbf{The cost of each succession policy} (\cref{sec:policies,sec:eval}).
  Six policies, from no succession to pre-authorized deputy activation, compared across
  three partition cases and three states of the commander, by how long the commander can
  record, how long nobody can, how many orders end up residual, and how many orders crews
  saw as valid before they turned residual.
  \item[(C3)] \textbf{Reconnection semantics} (\cref{sec:enforcement}). Appointments that
  take effect when the successor's device accepts them, and settlement of orders by the
  regional server, with two supporting results: an acknowledged timeout keeps one device
  at a time accepting orders whenever the gap between the deputy's and the commander's
  timeouts exceeds the clock error, even over store-and-forward links
  (\cref{prop:single-acceptor}); and settlement never revokes a confirmed verdict, but
  completes only through the predecessor's records or an act by the appointing officer
  (\cref{prop:settlement}).
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate on an executable model of the scenario of \cref{fig:scenario}, with a truth
oracle and \tbTotalRuns{} simulated incidents, including \tbSearchRuns{} with random link
flapping (\cref{sec:eval}). Among the results:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Under pre-authorized deputy activation, a commander's device that stops on its own
  can be revived by a heartbeat of the deputy's that a vehicle relays after the deputy has
  already taken over: in our witness the two record at once for a median of
  \tbRelayOverlap{}, and never with an acknowledged timeout.
  \item A takeover signed at the regional centre while the scene is cut off turns every
  order the commander gives in the meantime into a residual order when the links return:
  \tbSignedResidual{} orders, each already shown as valid on every crew device. If the
  takeover takes effect when the successor accepts it, none are.
  \item A lease renewed at the regional server, as in our earlier prototype, costs most when
  only the backhaul is lost: the commander is fit and reachable over the mesh, yet nobody
  can record orders for \tbPBNoCommand{} of a 1{,}200\,s outage.
\end{itemize}
