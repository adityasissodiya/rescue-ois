\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Organization Has to Choose}
\label{sec:org-choices}

The policies do not differ in whether they fail, but in where and at whose expense.
Leases that a device renews at the regional server protect against two commanders at the
price of having none when only the backhaul is lost, which the scenario suggests is the
common case; a timeout measured against the deputy (S4) avoids that price but cannot
detect an unfit commander whose device is still connected; a takeover by the duty chief
(S3) handles the unfit commander through voice and a report from the scene, but cannot reach
a scene that is cut off from the centre. Each choice has a parameter---$L$, $T$, the time to
report a commander unfit---that the organization, not the system designer, should set, and
each parameter's cost depends on how long commanders' data outages last. The evaluation
gives the shape of these costs; the rescue service has to supply the distributions that
turn them into expected costs. \pending{Interview questions in TCPS\_PLAN.md.}

Command given by voice alone is the hidden cost of policies that stop the commander. When a
device refuses its commander, the commander does not stop commanding: the orders go out by
radio and never enter the journal. The journal is then complete only in the sense that it
holds every order the system accepted.

\subsection{Presence of a Person, Not of a Device}
\label{sec:presence}

The scenario's ``cut off or incapacitated, and nobody can tell which'' is only half right.
The network cannot tell, and a device heartbeat actively says the wrong thing: it reports
that a device is up, not that its user can command. A succession rule that reacts to
silence therefore needs a signal of the person---a dead-man switch, a periodic
acknowledgement---and that signal will fire for a commander who is merely busy. The
human-factors literature on takeover requests has the same tension between alerting early
and alerting falsely~\cite{grindley2024handover}. \pending{Decision for the supervisors:
device or person presence; if person, what signal.}

\subsection{Designated and Plural Authority}
\label{sec:thesis}

This paper covers one class of decision: a command decision, which gets its legitimacy
from one designated person's role, takes effect when it is issued, and is settled by
authority, never by merge, when it conflicts with another. Decisions whose authority is
shared or contested belong to the opposite pole, where legitimacy comes from agreement
among several parties and a decision binds only at reconciliation; there, merge or
consensus is the right tool. The class of decision, not the state of the network, chooses
between the two. The model defines residual decisions, attributes and labels them; it does
not resolve them. Two successions in two parts of a partition (\cref{sec:eval-faults}) are
where the designated pole shades into the plural one.

\subsection{Security Assumptions}

The model assumes devices are not Byzantine and credentials are not stolen. A compromised
device of a legitimate person can still sign false acts, for example an activation that
claims a silence that did not happen, or a handover with a backdated timestamp. Signatures
make such acts attributable, not impossible. Heartbeats that carry a person's latest
sequence number may make backdating detectable: an act whose sequence number exceeds the
one a later heartbeat of the same person reported cannot have been signed when it claims.
\pending{Develop and evaluate this property, or state it as future work.} Revocation of a
stolen device is eventual under total disconnection.

\subsection{Limits of the Evidence}
\label{sec:limits}

The evaluation runs on a model of our own system, whose structural results---which policy
fails where, the counterexamples, the invariants---do not depend on its parameters, but
whose magnitudes do: latencies, gossip and heartbeat periods, reaction times and decision
rates are chosen, not measured. The truth oracle applies the same rule as the devices, so
it tests timing and clocks, not whether the rule is the right one for the organization.
Outages have fixed lengths; there is one incident, one commander and one deputy, a fixed
topology, and no mobility or radio model. The random search is evidence, not proof.
